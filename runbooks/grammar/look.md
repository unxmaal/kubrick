# Look: light and emphasis

Rules for lighting and framing as storytelling. Together with mcm RULE #155: always state the light and exposure in a look description, or the image model supplies its own. A source marked `secondary` reports a book we have not read.

## motivated-key
- kind: practice
- decision: Give the main light a source the scene could have: a lamp, a window, a doorway. For low-key mood, use one hard key with little fill.
- applies when: Writing the look of every shot and the light in every image prompt.
- check: Each shot's description names where its key light comes from.
- counterexample: A stylised, sourceless light used on purpose for a dream or memory.
- sources:
  - secondary: Bailey 2013, John Alton: Cinematography's Outlier, Part Two, theasc.com, on Alton, Painting with Light, chapters 3 and 5: a single hard key and little fill for low key, and light that comes from somewhere.
  - Saint Michael's College lecture, The Art of Lighting (2018): the key is usually motivated by a source in the scene.

## key-fill-ratio
- kind: provisional
- decision: Choose the key-to-fill ratio for mood: about 2:1 reads high key, 4:1 low key, 8:1 very low key. The ratio is key plus fill over fill.
- applies when: Describing the light of a shot or a still prompt.
- check: Look descriptions state a ratio or say high key, low key or very low key.
- counterexample: A flat, even light chosen on purpose for a clinical feel.
- confirm: The ASC Manual's section on lighting ratios, once the book arrives.
- sources:
  - UT Austin RTF 318 course wiki, Lighting ratios.

## frame-for-emotion
- kind: practice
- decision: Frame tighter to draw the viewer into a character's feeling, wider to let them stand back. Choose a shot's size for the emphasis the moment needs.
- applies when: Choosing a shot's scale in the shot list.
- check: Each close shot's description names the feeling or detail it brings the viewer close to.
- counterexample: A wide shot held at an emotional peak, to isolate a character, is a deliberate reversal.
- sources:
  - secondary: Hemmeter 1980, Framed by Hitchcock, Mise-en-Scene 2 (Case Western Reserve), pp. 2-10, on Hitchcock/Truffaut p. 43: the screen rectangle filled with emotion.
  - Svanera et al. 2018, Who is the director of this movie?, arXiv 1807.09560: shot scale sets emphasis and emotional involvement.

## suspense-over-surprise
- kind: provisional
- decision: Where the story allows, show the audience the danger before the characters know it, so the scene runs on suspense rather than a single surprise. Keep surprise for a twist the story pays off.
- applies when: Planning the order of information in a scene with a threat.
- check: The shot list shows the threat to the audience before the scene's characters react to it, or notes why surprise is wanted.
- counterexample: Hitchcock's own Psycho shower scene, a surprise by design.
- confirm: Hitchcock/Truffaut, p. 52 (1967 edition), once the book arrives.
- sources:
  - secondary: Bordwell 2013, Hitchcock, Lessing, and the bomb under the table, davidbordwell.net, on Hitchcock/Truffaut p. 52 and the interview tapes: the bomb-under-the-table account, traced back to Lessing, with Hitchcock's own exceptions.
